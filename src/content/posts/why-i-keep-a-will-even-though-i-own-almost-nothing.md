---
title: Why I Keep a Will Even Though I Own Almost Nothing
section: stewardship
dek: On stewardship as a habit of mind that starts long before there's an estate to speak of.
date: 2026-03-01
---

*This is a reflection on why the habit matters, not instructions for drafting anything. Estate documents are exactly the kind of thing you want a licensed attorney involved in, even — especially — when the estate in question is small.*

In my twenties I put off having a will drawn up for the obvious reason: I didn't have anything worth willing. A used car, a handful of books, some debt. It felt faintly absurd to pay a lawyer to formalize the disposition of essentially nothing, and for a while I treated that absurdity as a good reason to wait until I'd actually accumulated something. What changed my mind wasn't acquiring more. It was realizing that a will isn't really a document about your assets. It's a document about your responsibilities, and I had those already — a spouse, and later a child, and a set of decisions about guardianship and care that someone was going to have to make on my behalf the moment I couldn't make them myself.

The delay, I think, comes from imagining stewardship as something that kicks in once you cross a threshold of net worth — a concern for people with estates, in the old, slightly grand sense of that word. But the theological content of stewardship has never been about the size of the thing stewarded. The parable of the talents doesn't distinguish in moral weight between the servant given five talents and the one given two; both are held to the same standard of faithfulness with what they were actually given. A will is just the mundane, legal expression of that same faithfulness applied to whatever you happen to have, however modest — and to the people who depend on you, which is usually the part that actually matters most and the part people forget a will even covers.

There's a second thing I didn't expect: having the document forced a set of conversations I'd have otherwise put off indefinitely. Who would raise my daughter if my wife and I were both gone. What happens to the handful of things that matter for reasons no appraiser would ever capture — my grandfather's tools, a particular set of books. None of this required wealth to think through. It required sitting down and taking seriously that I already had things and people worth being deliberate about, which was true well before it felt true.

I don't think everyone needs to run out and hire an attorney this week for a two-page will covering a used car. But I'd gently push back on the instinct to wait until the estate feels big enough to justify the formality. The formality isn't really about the estate. It's about admitting, on paper, that you're already responsible for more than you've gotten around to planning for.
