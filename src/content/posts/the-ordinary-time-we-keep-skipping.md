---
title: The Ordinary Time We Keep Skipping
section: faith
dek: On the long green stretch of the liturgical calendar, and why the Church built in more boredom than we'd like.
date: 2026-04-05
---

By my count we are somewhere in the neighborhood of week eleven of Ordinary Time by the time this gets published, and I would bet a reasonable sum that most people reading this could not tell you which week without checking. That's not a criticism. Ordinary Time is designed to be the part of the year you don't track closely. It's the green vestments, the long stretch between the drama of Easter and the drama of Advent, the section of the calendar with no headline feast to anchor a homily around. And I think it's the part of the liturgical year we understand the least, precisely because we've decided in advance that it isn't for anything.

The word "ordinary" here doesn't mean unimportant, whatever it sounds like. It comes from the same root as "ordinal" — counted, numbered, given an order — and the Sundays of Ordinary Time are literally just numbered: the Eighth Sunday, the Ninth, the Tenth, marching forward with no particular occasion attached. That plainness is the content, not the absence of it. The Church, having spent Advent and Christmas and Lent and Easter walking you through the most concentrated parts of the story, spends the rest of the year teaching you to live inside the story at low intensity, which is where almost all of actual life happens.

I notice this most in my own prayer life, where I have a bad habit of saving my attention for the seasons that ask for it. Lent gets fasting and extra spiritual reading. Advent gets a wreath and a plan. Ordinary Time gets whatever's left over, which some years has been close to nothing. But the math doesn't work: Ordinary Time is roughly thirty-three or thirty-four weeks out of fifty-two, which means the "unmarked" season is the actual majority of the Christian year, and treating it as a placeholder between the important parts means treating most of my life that way too.

What I've started doing instead, with mixed success, is picking one small, unglamorous practice per stretch of Ordinary Time — a specific intercession, a specific book of Scripture read slowly, nothing that would make a good Instagram caption — and just keeping it going with no feast to build toward. It's not exciting. That's rather the point. The saints I actually admire were not, as far as I can tell, running on adrenaline from one liturgical high point to the next. They were doing something steady in the numbered weeks, which is most of the weeks there are.
