---
title: The Trade School Down the Road
section: vocation
dek: On the quiet return of apprenticeship, and what four years of debt-free plumbing school says about dignity and work.
date: 2026-08-11
---

A young man I know finished a plumbing apprenticeship this spring with no debt, a job waiting for him, and a starting wage that would take a lot of recent four-year graduates several years to reach. None of this surprised the tradespeople I know, who have been watching this shift for a while. What struck me was how much explaining I still find myself doing when I mention it — a kind of reflexive need, in certain rooms, to justify why a smart eighteen-year-old might choose a trade over a lecture hall. That reflex is the actual subject here, more than the plumbing.

The instinct to rank the trades beneath the professions has a long pedigree, and it isn't entirely wrong to notice where it comes from: a genuine tradition, going back through Aristotle, of prizing contemplation over the "servile arts." But that tradition also insisted, more carefully than we usually remember, that the servile arts were dignified in their own right — necessary, skilled, and morally serious — even while ranking contemplative life above them for reasons that had nothing to do with money or status. What we've done in the last few generations is keep the ranking and lose the dignity, treating trade work as a fallback for people who couldn't manage something better rather than as a genuine vocation with its own excellences to be pursued for their own sake.

Pope Leo XIII, writing at the birth of the modern labor question, was blunt about this: labor is not a commodity to be haggled down to its cheapest price, but the exercise of a human person's God-given capacities, deserving of a wage that reflects that dignity rather than mere market clearing. A good plumber, electrician, or welder is not performing a lesser act of reason than a good accountant. Both are exercising a real craft — a stable, reasoned disposition toward making or doing something well — and both deserve to be paid and regarded as such.

What I find hopeful about the current shift back toward trades isn't just the economics, real as those are. It's the possibility of recovering an older, truer sense of vocation, in which the question isn't "which path signals the most status" but "what work am I actually suited to do well, and does it serve people in some concrete way I can point to at the end of the day." A plumber ends most days having fixed something that was broken for someone. That's not a consolation prize. It's a full, coherent answer to what work is for.
