---
title: Subsidiarity Is Not a Talking Point
section: vocation
dek: On the difference between citing a principle and actually building something small enough to fail locally.
date: 2026-06-02
---

Subsidiarity gets invoked constantly in the circles I read — usually as a one-line objection to some distant authority overreaching, cited and dropped in the space of a sentence, as though naming the principle were the same as living by it. I've done this myself plenty of times. It's an easy principle to cite and a hard one to actually practice, because practicing it means giving up the efficiency and control that centralization offers, and most of us, including me, like efficiency and control more than we like admitting we like them.

The principle itself, as Pius XI stated it, is that it is an injustice for a larger and higher association to arrogate to itself functions which can be performed efficiently by smaller and lower associations. Notice what that actually requires in practice: not just tolerating small, local institutions as a sentimental preference, but a real, structural willingness to let them fail, learn, and improve on their own terms rather than stepping in the moment they underperform some centralized standard. Most invocations of subsidiarity I encounter skip straight past that requirement. People want the principle's conclusion — less centralized authority — without the principle's actual cost, which is tolerating a period of local mess while local competence develops.

I got a small, concrete lesson in this a couple of years ago helping start a men's group at our parish. My instinct, trained by every organization I'd ever worked in, was to write a detailed program, source materials from a national apostolate, and run it the way a franchise runs a store — consistent, controlled, replicable. A priest I trust talked me out of most of that, not because consistency is bad, but because a program handed down whole doesn't actually build the local capacity subsidiarity is meant to protect. What we did instead was slower and messier: a loose structure, real deference to the specific men who showed up and what they actually needed, and a willingness to let the first year be somewhat disorganized while the group found its own shape. It would have been easier, in the short run, to import someone else's finished answer.

That's the actual test of whether subsidiarity is a talking point or a practice: whether you're willing to let the smaller, closer institution be worse at first than the centralized alternative, for the sake of it eventually being more genuinely its own. Distributism and subsidiarity both ask for the same patience — tolerance for a slower, more local, occasionally clumsier process, in exchange for something that actually belongs to the people doing it rather than being administered to them from somewhere else.
