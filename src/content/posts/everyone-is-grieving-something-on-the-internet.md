---
title: Everyone Is Grieving Something on the Internet
section: culture
dek: On the public performance of mourning, and the older, quieter forms it replaced.
date: 2026-07-03
---

Every few weeks now some public death or public tragedy produces the same pattern: an enormous, fast wave of posts, a competitive quality to the grief that nobody quite admits is competitive, and then, within days, a near-total silence as everyone moves on to whatever comes next. I've done this myself, more than once, and each time I've noticed a small dishonesty in it that took me a while to name. It isn't that the grief is fake, exactly. It's that the form it's taking was never built to hold grief in the first place, and something gets lost in forcing it through that shape.

Public mourning used to have actual architecture: a wake, a funeral Mass, a wearing of black for a set period, a set of customs that told you how to grieve and, just as importantly, when to stop performing it and start simply living with it. Those forms were slow on purpose. They gave grief somewhere to go that wasn't immediate and wasn't purely private either — a shared, external structure that carried you through the worst of it without requiring you to invent the right response on the spot. What we have instead, for most losses that reach us secondhand, is a feed that rewards the fastest and most legible reaction and then, structurally, has nothing to offer once the algorithm moves on. The mourning doesn't conclude. It just stops being visible.

I don't think most people posting are being cynical. I think we're using the only tools we've got, and the tools we've got are built for immediacy, not for the slow work grief actually requires. The trouble is that the tool shapes the grief, not just the expression of it. A eulogy written to be read aloud once, in a room of people who knew the person, is a different act than a caption written to be seen by strangers within the hour — and I don't think it's an accident that the second kind tends to focus more on the poster's feelings than the person who died.

What I've started doing, unglamorously, is delaying. Not refusing to post, but waiting a day or two past the reflex, and using the delay to actually pray for the dead rather than immediately performing a reaction to their death — which sounds like a small distinction and turns out to be a large one. The Church's actual practices around death, the ones we've mostly stopped using even as practicing Catholics, were built for exactly this: something slower than a feed, and aimed at the dead rather than at an audience.
