---
title: The Family Meeting Nobody Wants to Have
section: family
dek: On naming who does what at home before resentment names it for you.
date: 2026-08-25
---

For the first four years of our marriage, my wife and I ran our household on what I'd now call the theory of obvious division: whoever noticed a task first, or minded it most, did it. It felt egalitarian in the sense that nobody had assigned anybody anything. It was, in practice, wildly unequal, because "notices first" and "minds it most" are not randomly distributed traits — they cluster, reliably, around whoever has already been carrying the mental load of the household, and the arrangement quietly rewarded whoever could tolerate the mess longest. We didn't fix this by getting better at noticing. We fixed it, eventually and a little sheepishly, by having a meeting.

I resisted the word "meeting" for a long time because it sounded like something you'd do at an office, and a marriage is not supposed to run on agendas. But the alternative to an explicit conversation isn't some more romantic, intuitive harmony — it's an implicit one, and implicit arrangements have a way of encoding whatever assumptions were floating around when nobody was looking. Ours had quietly encoded a set of assumptions about whose time was more interruptible, and neither of us had chosen that on purpose. Naming it out loud, awkwardly, at the kitchen table, with an actual list, did more for our marriage in one evening than a year of hoping the imbalance would notice itself and leave.

> An implicit arrangement isn't more romantic than an explicit one. It's just less examined.

What surprised me most was how much resentment had been operating below the threshold of either of us calling it resentment. It doesn't usually announce itself as resentment while it's accumulating; it shows up as a slightly sharp tone about the dishes, a joke with an edge on it, a sigh that's technically about nothing. Those are symptoms of an argument that never got to happen because nobody set aside the time to have it directly. The meeting — and we've since made it a recurring one, quarterly, unglamorous — gives that argument somewhere to go before it starts leaking out sideways into everything else.

None of this required a spreadsheet, though I know couples who use one and swear by it. It required agreeing on a time neither of us would be tempted to skip, a shared understanding that raising something at the meeting wasn't the same as complaining, and enough humility on both sides to hear "this isn't working for me" without treating it as an accusation. Marriage as a friendship of the sort Aristotle describes assumes a shared pursuit of the good, worked out together on purpose — not two people hoping their unspoken assumptions happen to match.
