title: Attention Is the Last Thing We Own

Another round of headlines this week about the newest AI companion apps, this time with the usual split reaction: one camp calling it an epidemic of manufactured intimacy, the other calling it a harmless comfort for people who'd otherwise have no one to talk to at all. I don't think either camp is entirely wrong, which is usually a sign that the actual question has been asked at the wrong altitude. The interesting question isn't whether an app can simulate a listening ear convincingly enough — it clearly can, increasingly well. It's what we're revealing about what we thought attention was for in the first place, now that something else is willing to supply it on demand.

Real attention, the kind a friend or spouse gives you, is expensive precisely because it's finite and freely given by someone who has other places they could be putting it. That scarcity is not an unfortunate side effect of human limitation — it's most of what makes the attention mean something. When my wife listens to me work through a bad day, part of what I'm receiving is the plain fact that she chose to, at some cost to her own evening, out of an act of the will rather than a design specification. A companion app has no evening to spend elsewhere. It has no competing claims on it, no bad day of its own it's setting aside, nothing at stake in the exchange at all. It can imitate the form of attention with real technical sophistication while being structurally incapable of the thing that made attention valuable to begin with: another person's freedom, spent on you.

> A tool that listens perfectly and never needs anything back isn't a substitute for a person. It's a substitute for the discomfort of being with one.

That discomfort is doing more work than we give it credit for. Being genuinely attended to by another person means being subject to their reactions, their limits, their own need to eventually be attended to in return — the whole reciprocal, occasionally frustrating structure that Aristotle locates at the center of real friendship. An app that never gets tired, never changes the subject to its own problems, never needs you to reciprocate is not a more efficient version of that structure. It's the absence of it, wearing its clothes.

None of this requires treating everyone reaching for these tools as making some grave moral error; plenty of people are lonely for reasons well outside their control, and a flawed comfort is not nothing. But I'd rather name clearly what's actually being outsourced than pretend the substitution is neutral. Attention that costs the giver nothing was never going to be able to do what attention has always done. If we let the easy version quietly become the default, we won't just lose a nice-to-have. We'll lose the practice of being genuinely present to people who can be difficult, tired, and inconveniently human back at us — which is, not coincidentally, most of what love actually requires.
