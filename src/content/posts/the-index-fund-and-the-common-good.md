---
title: The Index Fund and the Common Good
section: stewardship
dek: A distributist looks at passive investing and finds it more compatible with subsidiarity than expected — with limits.
date: 2026-05-11
---

*As with everything in this section: I'm describing a way of thinking, not recommending a portfolio. Talk to a licensed financial advisor about what, if anything, applies to your own situation.*

Distributists have a reputation, not entirely undeserved, for being suspicious of large, impersonal financial instruments on principle. Chesterton and Belloc were writing against a world of absentee shareholders and concentrated capital, and a broad-market index fund is, on its face, exactly that: money pooled into thousands of companies you'll never visit, managed by people you'll never meet, generating returns whose connection to any actual local good is nearly impossible to trace by hand. I expected, when I actually sat down to think this through rather than just feeling vaguely guilty about my retirement account, to end up somewhere critical. I ended up somewhere more mixed.

The case in favor is simpler than it looks. Ownership, even fractional and impersonal ownership, is not nothing — it's still better than the alternative most people actually face, which is no ownership at all and total dependence on a wage with no stake in the enterprise producing it. Distributism's actual target was never diversified small ownership; it was concentration, the condition where capital pools in a few hands while everyone else works for wages with no claim on what they help produce. An index fund, whatever else you say about it, spreads a claim on productive capital across an enormous number of ordinary households instead of leaving it walled up in a handful of family trusts. That's not the local, personal, subsidiarity-respecting ideal Belloc had in mind, but it's a real distance from the thing he was actually warning against.

Where I get more uneasy is further out, past the fund itself and into what it's actually funding. An index fund doesn't ask whether a company treats its workers well, pays suppliers fairly, or builds anything durable; it just tracks the market, which rewards those things and their opposites with equal indifference. Passive investing is, in that sense, a strange kind of moral abdication dressed up as prudence — you get the diversification and the low fees, and in exchange you stop asking what, exactly, your money is doing while you're not looking.

What I've settled on for my own household is not a clean answer, which is probably the honest place to land. The bulk of long-term saving still sits in broad index funds, because the alternative of trying to hand-pick virtuous companies with my own limited judgment and limited capital is its own kind of hubris. But I've started treating a smaller portion — deliberately, not out of guilt — as a place to actually exercise judgment: a local credit union, a friend's small business, a cooperative that answers to the people who use it rather than to distant shareholders. Subsidiarity doesn't require refusing the large tool. It requires not letting the large tool become the only one in the box.
