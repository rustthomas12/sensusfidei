---
title: Raising Children Who Will Leave the Table
section: family
dek: On forming kids for a life you won't get to supervise, starting with dinner.
date: 2026-06-30
---

Somewhere around my daughter's third birthday it occurred to me, with the particular force reserved for facts you already knew abstractly, that I am raising someone who will eventually eat dinner every night for the rest of her life without me at the table. Most of parenting, when I actually look at it honestly, is like this: not the production of a permanent state but the handoff of a set of habits to a person who's going to keep running them long after supervision ends. The dinner table happens to be where I notice it most, because it's the one formation I'm engaged in on a literal daily basis.

What we're actually forming at dinner isn't table manners, though we do plenty of that. It's a set of much bigger habits wearing manners as a disguise: the habit of waiting for everyone before starting, which is a small daily lesson in not treating your own hunger as the only hunger in the room. The habit of saying grace, which is a small daily lesson in receiving things rather than assuming they're simply owed to you. The habit of staying at the table through a conversation that isn't about you, which is nearly the whole of what it means to be a decent adult at any gathering later in life. None of these get explained to a three-year-old in those terms, obviously. They get repeated, daily, without much comment, until they're load-bearing.

This is Aristotle's account of virtue formation more than it's a parenting theory I invented: you become just by doing just acts, become temperate by doing temperate acts, and the habits get laid down before the understanding catches up to explain them. A child doesn't wait for kindness at the table because she's worked out an ethical argument for patience. She waits because waiting is what happens at this table, repeated enough times that it becomes what she does rather than a rule she's following. The understanding, if it comes, comes later, and it comes easier because the habit already built the groove for it to run in.

I don't think this means every dinner needs to be a seminar, and ours mostly aren't — most nights it's noise and spilled water and someone narrating their day in exhausting detail. But I've stopped treating the table as neutral time that happens to also involve food, and started treating it as one of the only daily, repeated, low-stakes opportunities I actually have to practice her into the kind of adult who can sit through an uninteresting conversation, wait her turn, and receive a meal she didn't make as a gift rather than a given. She won't remember most of these dinners specifically. That was never really the point.
