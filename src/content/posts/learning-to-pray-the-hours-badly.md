---
title: Learning to Pray the Hours Badly
section: faith
dek: On starting the Liturgy of the Hours with a two-year-old underfoot, and why the stumbling matters more than the schedule.
date: 2026-08-02
---

I started praying Morning Prayer again in January, which means I have now failed to pray it properly for eight months in a row. Some days it's the antiphon I skip because I can't find the ribbon fast enough. Some days it's the whole thing, said in the car, badly, with a psalm interrupted twice by a request for a different snack. I used to think this counted as not really doing it. I've come around to thinking it's closer to the point than the version I had in my head, which involved silence, a good chair, and a version of myself who doesn't exist yet.

The Liturgy of the Hours was never built for hermits, whatever the image on the holy card suggests. It was built to be prayed in choir, by people with jobs and colds and arguments they were still having under their breath, and then it was handed down to the rest of us on the theory that ordinary life could be hung on the same scaffolding. The hours don't ask you to feel anything in particular. They ask you to show up at roughly the right time and say roughly the right words, and they trust that the saying will do something the feeling can't be relied on to do.

What surprised me is how little the interruptions seem to matter to the prayer itself, even when they matter enormously to me. The psalm doesn't stop meaning what it means because I said half of it in a parking lot. Augustine has a line I keep coming back to, about praying with groans too deep for words — and a toddler's negotiation over goldfish crackers is not exactly a groan, but it's not nothing either. It's the actual texture of the day I'm supposed to be sanctifying, not a competing thing to get past before the real prayer starts.

> The hours don't ask you to feel anything in particular. They ask you to show up.

I don't want to make this sound tidier than it is. Most mornings I still finish Lauds a little annoyed at myself, aware of how much of it I said on autopilot. But I've stopped taking that as evidence I'm doing it wrong. The monks had bells and a whole architecture of silence built around them, and even they complained about distraction constantly — you can read about it in their own writing, going back seventeen centuries. If the men who invented this practice couldn't keep their minds from wandering, I don't think the goal was ever a wandering-free hour. The goal was fidelity to the hour itself: showing up again tomorrow, ribbon or no ribbon, snack negotiation or no snack negotiation, and letting eight months of badly-prayed hours add up to something a single good one never could.
