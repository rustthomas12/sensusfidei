*A note before this one: I'm not a financial advisor, an accountant, or an attorney, and nothing below is advice about your estate, your taxes, or your particular family's situation. If you're dealing with an actual inheritance, please talk to someone licensed who can look at your specifics. What follows is a way of thinking about the thing, not a plan for handling it.*

When my grandfather died he left a small piece of land that had been in the family for two generations, along with the kind of modest savings that get called "an inheritance" mostly out of politeness. I remember the first calculation I made, quietly, in the days after the funeral: what is this worth, and what would I do with that much money. I'm not proud of how fast that calculation arrived, and I don't think I'm unusual in that. Our culture has exactly one category for money that shows up unearned, and it's the lottery-winner category — a windfall, something you get to spend on yourself because nobody can say you didn't deserve the fun of it.

An inheritance is not that, and treating it that way is a category error with real consequences. A windfall asks one question — what do I want to do with this? An inheritance asks a second question first — what was this for? My grandfather didn't save that money or hold that land in a vacuum; he held it as a steward of something that was going to outlast him, same as I am now supposed to be a steward of it rather than its final owner. Catholic social teaching has a name for this that's easy to say and hard to actually absorb: the universal destination of goods. Private property is real and I'm not arguing against it. But it's held in trust toward the common good, and inherited property comes with that trusteeship already attached, whether or not a will spells it out.

Practically, this has changed less about what I do with the land and more about how I think about doing it. I still get to make decisions — sell it, keep it, work it, lease it — and none of those decisions is automatically the pious one. But I've stopped asking only what maximizes my own position and started asking what the last two generations were actually building toward, and whether my choice extends that or just liquidates it for my own convenience. Sometimes the answer is that liquidating it is genuinely the right call — debts get paid, needier relatives get helped, a family business that was already dying gets allowed to die with dignity instead of dragged along. The point isn't a fixed rule about keeping everything forever. It's asking the second question before reaching for the calculator that only knows how to ask the first one.
