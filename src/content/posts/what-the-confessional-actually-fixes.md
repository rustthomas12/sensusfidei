title: What the Confessional Actually Fixes

A friend asked me recently why I still go to confession when I could just as easily "process things" with a therapist or, failing that, a long walk. It's a fair question, and I don't think the honest answer is that confession is more efficient. It usually isn't. You wait in a line. You say the same formula you've said a hundred times. You are, more often than not, mildly bored in the fifteen minutes beforehand and mildly relieved in the five minutes after, and none of it feels like a breakthrough. The value isn't in the experience. It's in the structure underneath the experience, which does something a conversation, however good, can't do on its own.

Explaining a sin and confessing it are two different postures toward the same fact, and only one of them ends with someone else absolving you of it. Explaining puts the thing in context — here's what was going on, here's what I was afraid of, here's the pattern I'm working on. All of that is true and useful and I do plenty of it, in confession and out of it. But context is not absolution. You can understand a debt perfectly and still owe it. What confession adds, and what I don't think anything else quite replicates, is a moment where the accounting actually closes: a real person, standing in for something outside both of you, says the words that end the matter. Not "that makes sense" but "you are absolved."

I used to find the specificity requirement — naming the sin, not just the feeling around it — a little embarrassing, and I think that embarrassment was doing something useful I didn't appreciate at the time. It's easy to talk in the language of struggles and patterns indefinitely without ever landing on the plain sentence: I lied to my wife about the money. Saying the plain sentence out loud to another human being is uncomfortable in a way that journaling about "communication issues" is not, and the discomfort is not a design flaw. It's the whole mechanism. Aquinas talks about confession as a kind of judgment you volunteer for, and volunteering for judgment turns out to require actual charges, not just an atmosphere of remorse.

None of this makes the other conversations worthless. I still take the long walk. I still, when I need it, sit across from a good therapist and work through the pattern underneath the sentence. But I've stopped treating those as substitutes for the sentence itself. They explain the debt. Only one thing I know of actually closes it.
