---
title: A Book Gets Banned Somewhere Every Week Now
section: culture
dek: On the fight over what children should read, and why both sides are having the wrong argument.
date: 2026-05-19
---

Another school district, another list of contested titles, another round of the same two press releases: one side calling it censorship, the other calling it protecting children, both sides treating the fight as though it were about the books themselves. Having watched a decade of these fights go by, I've come to think the books are almost incidental. What's actually being fought over is a question neither side wants to state plainly, because stating it plainly would require admitting the other side isn't crazy: who gets to form a child's moral imagination, and by what authority.

The "protect the children" side is not wrong that formation is happening, constantly, through what a child reads, and that adults are responsible for it. Aristotle would recognize the instinct immediately — you become what you're habituated toward, and a child's imagination is being habituated by every story that gets inside it, whether or not an adult chose the story on purpose. Pretending that content is neutral, that a child absorbing a book is just "exposure to ideas" with no formative weight, is its own kind of naivety, and I don't think most people actually believe it when they say it. The "censorship" side is not wrong either that removing books wholesale, especially on thin pretexts, tends to teach a different and also bad lesson: that certain questions are too dangerous to be encountered even in fiction, which usually just guarantees the child encounters them later with less guidance, not more.

What both sides skip past is the actual, harder work underneath the fight, which is parents and communities exercising real, active judgment about formation rather than outsourcing it either to a school library's default shelf or to a ban list drawn up by people who've read excerpts rather than books. A library shelf is not a neutral commons; someone chose what's on it, using some set of values, whether examined or not. And a ban list is not protection; it's a different, cruder form of the same editorial choice, made at a distance from any particular child's actual needs.

I don't have a tidy policy prescription here, and I'm suspicious of anyone who does, because the actual unit of judgment is a household, not a district. What I do think is worth saying is that both fights, as currently conducted, let adults avoid the genuinely difficult work: reading the books yourself, talking with your own children about what's in them, and forming their imagination on purpose rather than fighting a proxy war over what gets left on a shelf you were never going to personally curate anyway.
